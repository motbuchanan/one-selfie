The first test

Same shirt. Different room.

Before I asked it to change anything, I asked it to change nothing. Same face, same gray henley, same smile. Just move me into a studio with a dark backdrop and real light.

Drag the slider. If that one didn't hold, nothing after it would. It held well enough to keep going.

How it actually went

Eleven days, two tools, a lot of no.

None of this was one magic prompt. It was a back and forth between me, Claude, and Grok, and most of the work was deciding what was wrong with a picture and how to say so.

Apr 5

The selfie

Phone camera, living room, window light. Not taken for this. It was just the best clear picture of my face I had.

May 5

First tests, and the drift

Early outputs came back as three slightly different guys who all kind of looked like me. Hair texture, face width, and the glasses kept wandering. That's the core problem with this whole idea, and I saw it on day one.

May 6 to 12

Writing the description of me

Claude and I built a written anchor: height, build, hair, frames, skin, coloring. It goes into every single prompt, word for word. Short prompts drifted. Long, boring, repetitive ones held.

May 13

The big batch

Formal, smart casual, outdoor, lifestyle. Tux, linen suit, a rooftop, a coastline, an autumn park. Run, look, critique, rewrite, run again.

May 16

Home

The last gap was ordinary life. A kitchen in the morning. Coffee across from someone who isn't in the frame. These ended up being my favorites.

Correction one: color

I had my colors done. So I used them.

I'm a True Spring. Warm, clear, golden. Navy, black, charcoal, and burgundy all fight my skin, and the first batch was full of them.

So the palette became a rule: camel, rust, olive, coral, cream, cognac. Warm light only. Kodak Portra on everything. That one change is most of why the library looks like it belongs together.

Correction two: my actual body

It kept making me smaller.

I'm 6'3" and about 235. Left alone, the model either squashed me into a chair or handed me a gym body I don't have. Both were wrong, and both were obvious to anyone who knows me.

The fix was saying it plainly: "slightly heavyset but proportionate for frame." Standing poses, real numbers, no flattery. That line fixed it, and it has been in every prompt since.

Who did what

The tools didn't pick the pictures.

Me

Taste and the word no

  • Decided what the library was for and what was missing from it
  • Rejected anything that didn't look like me, or looked like a better-looking stranger
  • Brought the color season, the real height and weight, and the photographer's eye for light
  • Picked every frame on this page out of a much bigger pile
Claude

The thinking partner

  • Turned my notes into the written description of me
  • Wrote and rewrote the prompts, fully self-contained
  • Critiqued each batch with me and named what was off
  • Built this page
Grok

The camera

  • Generated every image from the selfie plus a prompt
  • Got it right sometimes, wrong a lot, and occasionally better than I asked
Where it broke

The honest part.

Face driftEven with the anchor, some frames come back as a cousin. Those got thrown out, and there were plenty.
GlassesLens reflections and frame shapes are hard. Check the glasses first in any AI portrait and you'll see it.
HandsStill the giveaway. Hands in pockets and hands around mugs were partly a styling choice and partly a dodge.
It's always autumnWarm light and fall leaves suit my coloring, so the library leans hard on golden hour. Spring and summer are next.
Only one face testedEverything here is me. I haven't run this process on someone with a different face, skin, or coloring yet, and I won't call it a service until I have.
Not unique anymoreApps now do selfie-to-headshot for about $30. What they don't do is art direction: a palette, a story, someone saying no. That's the part worth anything.
So what is this?

An experiment that taught me to direct.

I'm a photographer and video person by trade, and I started this out of curiosity. What I got out of it is a working method for art-directing AI imagery the way I'd direct a real shoot: light, palette, wardrobe, story, and a lot of retakes.

If you need lifestyle imagery of a real person and a studio day isn't realistic, or you just want to talk through how this works, I'd like to hear about it.
